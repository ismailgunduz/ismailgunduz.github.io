BudgetSync

A shared budgeting app for couples and households, designed in Figma and built in Flutter with Claude Code.

RoleDesigner & builder
TimelineAug 2026 – now
PlatformiOS · Android
StackFlutter · Supabase

Project summary & challenges

BudgetSync is my own product. One household shares one budget and one list of expenses, and everyone in it sees the same numbers. Splitting costs with people outside the house, like a dinner or a trip, lives in a separate Planner with debts and settle-up. I designed it in Figma and built it in Flutter and Supabase, pairing with Claude Code. It is in testing and not in the stores yet.

The challenge

  • Shared money is personal. The app had to make one pooled budget feel fair, without turning a couple into creditors.
  • Logging has to be faster than not logging. Every extra tap is a reason to stop.
  • Numbers must never claim more than they know: a past month has nothing “left”, a trend line stops at today.

My role

  • Product: the household model, the free and paid line, the flows and requirements
  • Design: Figma screens, the app icon and every state in the app
  • Build: the Flutter app and Supabase backend, written with Claude Code as my pair programmer
  • Testing: 95 test files and a review brief for outside feedback

#01

Figma first

Design first, then code

Every screen started in Figma before a line of code: a dark interface with a violet header card, category chips with their own colours, and one big number at the top of every screen. The built app keeps that look, and changes only where real use showed a better way.

Figma designs

#02

Home

What is left, and what was spent

Home leads with the one number that matters this month: what is left of the budget. Below it, categories and the latest expenses, with who paid each one.

Go back a month and the header changes from “left” to “spent”, because a past month has no budget left to claim. Small wording, but it keeps the number honest.

#03

Add an expense

Fewer taps, the right defaults

Pick one of 14 categories, type the amount in a panel that sits above the keyboard, and save. Date defaults to today, the payer to you, and the subcategory to “other” so nobody is asked a question they have already answered. The budget and the list update at once, and deleting asks first.

#04

Insights

Donut, year and an honest trend

Insights shows the month or the year as a donut by category, with the saving on top and each category’s share below. The spending trend is cumulative and drawn against the budget line, and it stops at today instead of guessing the rest of the month. Any range can be picked, and the trend can be narrowed to one category.

#05

Planner

Trips and dinners, then settle up

Inside a household money is pooled, so there are no debts. Debts belong to the Planner: a trip, a wedding dinner, friends who are not in your house. Each event has participants with roles, a list of who paid what, and a settlement summary that shows who owes whom.

#06

Household & data

Invite, export, leave cleanly

One code brings people into the household, and the person who invites pays while the people who join pay nothing. Analytics is a switch you can turn off. Exporting all your data is always free, and deleting the account explains what will happen to your data before it happens.

#07

App icon

A B with two chevrons

The icon is my own drawing: a B with two chevrons, on the same violet as the app, with a white letter so it stays readable at home-screen size.

#08

Built in code

Design decisions as commits

Because I build the app myself, design changes ship as code the same day. Every commit says what changed for the person using the app. A few, word for word:

  • Tell people what they spent, not what they didn’t
  • Put a dot where money was spent, not on every day after
  • Stop asking for the date and the first tap
  • Show who paid and what for, in the same glance
  • Offer next month’s rent instead of making them type it again
  • Stop the trend line in the days that have happened

Looking back

What building it myself changed

Owning the code meant no hand-off: when a label claimed more than it knew, I fixed the label and the logic together. The open question before launch is the line between free and paid, and whether couples expect to settle up with each other too. That is what the outside review is for.
